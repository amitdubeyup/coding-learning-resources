# Behavioral interviews

Behavioral rounds decide more senior offers than people expect. At staff/principal
level the coding bar is assumed; what's really being tested is **judgment, scope of
impact, and how you operate with other people.** This guide gives you the method
and the structure — the stories have to be yours.

## What's actually being tested

Interviewers map your answers to a rubric. Behind every question they're scoring:
- **Ownership** — did you drive it, or were you carried along?
- **Impact & scope** — team? org? company? Quantified?
- **Judgment under ambiguity** — how you decide with incomplete information.
- **Collaboration & influence** — especially *without authority*.
- **Self-awareness & growth** — can you name a real failure and what changed after.

A technically correct story with no ownership, no conflict, and no lesson scores
poorly. Tension and your specific role in resolving it are the point.

## STAR, done properly

Structure every story as **S**ituation → **T**ask → **A**ction → **R**esult, and
add **L** (Learning) for failure/growth questions.

- **Situation (10%)** — just enough context. Two sentences, not five minutes.
- **Task (10%)** — *your* specific responsibility and the stakes.
- **Action (60%)** — the majority. Use "I", not "we". What *you* decided and did,
  the trade-offs you weighed, how you handled pushback.
- **Result (20%)** — quantified outcome + what you learned.

**Weak:** "We migrated to microservices and it went well."
**Strong:** "Our monolith's deploys took 2 hours and blocked the team *(S)*. I owned
the decomposition *(T)*. I proposed a strangler-fig migration, sequenced the five
highest-churn services first, and got two skeptical senior engineers on board by
running a two-week spike to de-risk it *(A)*. Deploys dropped to 15 minutes and
incident MTTR fell ~40%; the lesson was that sequencing by change-frequency, not by
dependency graph, was what actually unblocked the team *(R/L)*."

The difference is entirely in the **A** and the specificity.

## The competency map

Prepare stories that cover these eight dimensions. Most questions are a rephrasing
of one of them:

1. **Leadership / ownership** — drove an initiative end-to-end.
2. **Conflict / disagreement** — with a peer, and separately with a manager.
3. **Failure** — a real one, owned, with what changed afterward.
4. **Ambiguity** — decided and acted without clear requirements.
5. **Influence without authority** — moved a decision across teams you don't own.
6. **Delivery under pressure** — a trade-off between speed and quality/scope.
7. **Mentorship / growing others** — made someone else better.
8. **Handling feedback / a mistake** — took criticism and adjusted.

## Level expectations: senior → staff → principal

The *same question* is scored against different expected scope:

| | Senior | Staff | Principal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blast radius of your stories | your team | multiple teams / a domain | org / company strategy |
| Ambiguity | solve a hard problem | define which problem to solve | reframe whether it's the right problem |
| Influence | within the team | across teams via credibility | shift org direction & set technical strategy |
| "Result" that lands | shipped a system | changed how a domain works | changed an outcome the business tracks |

If you're targeting principal, don't tell senior-scoped stories. Choose examples
where you set direction, aligned multiple stakeholders, and moved a metric leaders
care about.

## Company frameworks

**Amazon** is the most explicit: every answer is scored against its **Leadership
Principles** — Customer Obsession, Ownership, Invent and Simplify, Are Right (A
Lot), Learn and Be Curious, Hire and Develop the Best, Insist on the Highest
Standards, Think Big, Bias for Action, Frugality, Earn Trust, Dive Deep, Have
Backbone; Disagree and Commit, Deliver Results, plus the newer *Strive to be
Earth's Best Employer* and *Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility*. Prepare
2+ stories per relevant LP; "Disagree and Commit" and "Dive Deep" come up constantly.

Others are less formal but similar in spirit: **Google** looks at "Googleyness" +
general cognitive ability + leadership + role-related knowledge; **Meta** probes
impact and moving fast; **Netflix** tests judgment against its culture memo
("freedom & responsibility"). Research the specific company's values and map your
stories to them before the loop.

## Build your story bank

Don't memorize scripts — build a **matrix** once and reuse it:

1. List **8–12 real stories** from your career (projects, incidents, conflicts,
   launches, failures).
2. For each, write S/T/A/R in bullet form with **hard numbers**.
3. Tag each story with the competencies it demonstrates. Aim for coverage — most
   good stories cover 2–3.
4. In the interview, map the question → competency → your best-fit story.

A strong story is reusable across many questions; you need range, not volume.

## Question bank by competency

**Ownership / leadership** — Tell me about a project you drove end-to-end. A time
you took ownership of something outside your remit. The most impactful thing you've
shipped.

**Conflict / disagreement** — A disagreement with a peer, and how you resolved it.
A time you disagreed with your manager or a senior leader. A time you were
overruled — what did you do next?

**Failure** — A project that failed or missed its goal. A decision you got wrong. A
time you received hard critical feedback.

**Ambiguity** — A time you had to act without clear requirements. How you made a
call with incomplete data. Starting something with no established process.

**Influence without authority** — Getting other teams to adopt your approach.
Driving a technical decision across an org. Aligning stakeholders who disagreed.

**Delivery under pressure** — A speed-vs-quality trade-off. A time you were behind
schedule. Handling a major incident/outage.

**Mentorship** — Growing an underperforming engineer. Making the team better beyond
your own output.

**These reuse across levels** — scale the *scope* of the example to the level.

## The hard ones

- **"Tell me about a failure."** Pick a *real* one with real stakes; own your part
  without blaming others; spend most of the time on what you changed afterward. A
  fake "my weakness is I work too hard" answer fails instantly.
- **"A time you disagreed with your manager."** Show you pushed with data, then
  either changed their mind *or* disagreed-and-committed gracefully. Never "I was
  right and they finally admitted it."
- **"Influence without authority."** The gold-standard senior story: no positional
  power, so you won on evidence, relationships, and a de-risking proof-of-concept.
- **"Handle ambiguity."** Show a bias for action: you gathered what you could,
  made a reversible bet, and adjusted — not analysis paralysis.

## Red flags

Interviewers down-score when they hear: "we" with no clear personal role; blaming
teammates/management; no metrics; no conflict or tension; no lesson learned;
rambling with no structure; or a "failure" that's secretly a humblebrag. Also
avoid over-claiming — inconsistent or exaggerated stories collapse under the
follow-up "dive deep" questions.

## "Tell me about yourself" & questions to ask them

**The opener** — 60–90 seconds, present → past → why-this-role. A neutral template:

> "I'm a [role] focused on [domain/strength]. Most recently I [one flagship
> achievement with a metric]. Before that I [one line of relevant background]. I'm
> looking to [why this team/role] because [genuine motivation]."

Fill it with *your* facts. It's a headline, not your résumé read aloud.

**Ask them** (signals seniority and genuine interest): What does success look like
in this role at 6 and 12 months? What's the hardest technical problem the team is
facing? How are technical decisions made and disagreements resolved? What does the
path from this level to the next look like here?
